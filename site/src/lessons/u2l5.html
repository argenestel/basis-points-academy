Ethereum's single-threaded EVM is one point in a large design space, and the most interesting DeFi infrastructure of 2026 often sits elsewhere in it. This lesson surveys the main alternatives, not for trivia but because the choice of execution environment determines what you can build cheaply: a high-frequency order book is painful on Ethereum and natural on an appchain, and knowing why is the difference between fighting your platform and fitting it.

Solana: parallelism by declaration

Solana is one global state machine optimised for throughput and latency. Its key trick: every transaction declares, up front, which accounts it will read and write. The runtime (Sealevel) can then run any two transactions that don't touch the same accounts in parallel, because they can't conflict. Slots are about 400ms. And because contention is per-account, Solana has local fee markets: heavy demand for one hot account (a popular pool or mint) raises fees for transactions touching that account, without raising fees for everyone, unlike Ethereum's global base fee (lesson 3). A second validator client (Firedancer) and a new consensus protocol (Alpenglow, approved by validators in 2025, targeting much faster finality) are pushing its performance further. Units 23 and 24 are devoted to building on it.

Move: assets that can't be duplicated

Move (Aptos, Sui) takes a different angle: make whole classes of bug impossible in the language. Assets are resources, a type that cannot be copied and cannot be silently dropped. You can't accidentally duplicate a token or lose one; the compiler won't let you. A lot of Solidity's footguns (double-spends from a logic error, tokens stranded by a missing transfer) are type errors in Move. Aptos runs Block-STM for parallelism: execute transactions optimistically in parallel, detect where they actually conflicted, and re-execute just those. Monad brings the same optimistic-parallel approach to the EVM, so you get Ethereum compatibility with parallel execution.

Three answers to the same question

"How do you run an onchain order book?" has at least three good 2026 answers, each making a different trade:

  • Hyperliquid: its own L1 (HyperBFT, a HotStuff variant) with a native order-book engine (HyperCore) and an EVM (HyperEVM) so third parties can still compose. Maximum control, you build and secure the chain.
  • dYdX v4: a Cosmos appchain where validators run in-memory order books and settle on chain. Fast, with the Cosmos SDK doing the heavy lifting (Unit 3).
  • Lighter: a ZK rollup that proves its matching is correct, so users don't have to trust the operator's ordering. Trust-minimised, at the cost of prover complexity (Unit 4).

Notice the axis: all three want fast, fair ordering, and they differ in how much they build themselves versus inherit, and how much trust they remove with cryptography versus consensus. The same axis runs through every "should I use a framework or roll my own" decision in Unit 3.
