A user never reads your smart contract; they read your interface and sign what it shows them. That makes the frontend part of the protocol's attack surface, and some of the largest losses in crypto came not from contract bugs but from compromised frontends serving malicious transactions to users who trusted what they saw (Unit 5's Bybit). This lesson is about building interfaces that can't be turned against the people using them.

The frontend is attack surface

Three ways an attacker gets between the user and the honest contract:

  • DNS and registrar hijacks: attackers took over front-end domains at Curve (2022) and Balancer (2023), and front-end compromises were reported at Aerodrome and Velodrome. Each served pages that asked users for drainer approvals, the contract was fine, the page was not.
  • Supply-chain attacks: a malicious version of a widely-used library (Ledger Connect Kit, December 2023) briefly injected a drainer into many dApps at once. You didn't have to be hacked; your dependency was (Unit 15).
  • Manipulated signing UIs: Bybit's signers approved a malicious transaction shown through a compromised Safe interface (Feb 2025, ~$1.5B). They saw something legitimate; the transaction was something else.

Show the user what will actually happen

The single highest-leverage frontend defence is to simulate every transaction and show the result in plain words: "you send 1 ETH, you receive at least 2,946 USDC." If the simulation shows something different from what the user intended, "you grant unlimited approval to 0xUnknown", they can stop before signing. This is the frontend version of Unit 18 lesson 2's simulate-before-send, turned into a safety feature for the user. Combined with readable signing (EIP-712 so the wallet shows structured data, not a hash) and scoped approvals, it means a user signing through your interface sees the truth of what they're authorising, which is exactly what the Bybit signers didn't.

Build it

Extend the dapp-viem lab: before any write, check the chain id and that the target address's bytecode hash matches a pinned value; show a plain-language summary of the simulated balance changes; and refuse unlimited approvals. Building the bytecode-hash check and the plain-language simulation summary is the frontend-security mindset made concrete: you're treating your own interface as untrusted and verifying, on the client, that the user is interacting with the contract you expect and authorising only what they intend. That's the discipline that would have caught the swapped-contract and unlimited-approval attacks.
