In 2017 nobody thought you could run an exchange without an order book. Order books need market makers, market makers need to update quotes constantly, and on a chain where every update costs gas and lands seconds later, nobody would. Uniswap's answer was to replace the market maker with a formula: a pool of two tokens that will trade with anyone, at any size, at a price the formula sets. This lesson derives that formula, prices a real swap through it, and sets up the question the next lesson answers: who pays for a quote that's always available?

The invariant

A constant-product pool holds reserves x of token X and y of token Y. The rule is: any trade is allowed as long as the product of the reserves does not go down.

x · y = k marginal price of X in units of Y = y / x

The marginal price is the price of an infinitesimally small trade. Any real trade gets a worse price than that, and the formula says exactly how much worse.

Why y/x? If the pool holds 1,000 ETH and 2,000,000 USDC, the ratio of what it has says ETH is worth $2,000 to it. Take a tiny amount of ETH out and the pool must receive about $2,000 to keep k constant. Take a lot out and, because k is a product, each additional ETH costs more than the last. That curvature is the entire mechanism.

Deriving the swap

You send dx of X into the pool and want dy of Y out. A fee f (0.3% in Uniswap v2) is taken from your input first, so only dx·(1 − f) counts toward the invariant.

dx_eff = dx · (1 − f) (x + dx_eff) · (y − dy) = x · y dy = y · dx_eff / (x + dx_eff)

The full dx (including the fee) is added to the reserves, so k actually grows a little on every trade. That growth is the LPs' fee income.

Two rules of thumb fall out. A trade equal to fraction p of the reserve (here 1%) pays about p in price impact, and leaves the marginal price moved by about 2p. Both are worth memorising: they tell you in your head how deep a pool is.

Here is Uniswap v2's implementation of the same line, with the fee folded in as 997/1000 integer arithmetic:

uint amountInWithFee = amountIn * 997;
uint numerator   = amountInWithFee * reserveOut;
uint denominator = reserveIn * 1000 + amountInWithFee;
amountOut = numerator / denominator;   // rounds down: in the pool's favour

Notice the rounding. Every division in an AMM rounds toward the pool. Unit 5 and the Balancer incident in lesson 4 are what happens when one doesn't.

Providing liquidity

LPs deposit both tokens in the pool's current ratio and receive shares (LP tokens) representing a pro-rata claim on the reserves.

first deposit: shares = √(x · y) (Uniswap v2 also burns the first 1,000 shares) later deposits: shares = totalShares · min(dx / x, dy / y) withdrawal: you get shares / totalShares of each reserve

√(x·y) makes the share count independent of which token you call X. The 1,000 burned shares are the first (crude) defence against the share-inflation attack in Unit 5.

Because the whole fee is added to reserves, k rises with every trade while the number of shares stays fixed. Each share therefore claims a growing k. That is how LPs are paid: not in a separate fee balance, but in the slowly increasing value of their claim.

That looks wonderful, and it would be if it were the whole story. The next lesson shows what the LP gave up to earn it, and it is often more than 55%.

Why it works at all

Three properties made this design take over DeFi.

  • It always quotes. For any size, in either direction, with no counterparty to find. The price gets worse as size grows, but it never says no.
  • It needs no operator. No one updates quotes. The pool's price is corrected by whoever notices it's wrong and profits from fixing it: the arbitrageur.
  • It composes. Any contract can swap against it in the same transaction as something else, which is what makes flash loans, liquidations and aggregators possible.

The second property is the catch. "Corrected by arbitrageurs" means the pool is always trading at a stale price against someone who knows the current one. In the language of Unit 1, the LP is a market maker who never updates quotes, and the arbitrageur is the informed trader. The 55% in the table is the spread income.
