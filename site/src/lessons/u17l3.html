There is a wide, dangerous gap between a protocol that works on your fork and one that safely holds strangers' money, and the launch checklist is how you cross it. This lesson collects everything from the security (Unit 15), economic (Unit 15), liquidity (Unit 13) and legal (Units 8, 10, 13) threads into one pre-launch and post-launch checklist. It's the least glamorous lesson in the course and the one that most determines whether your protocol ends up in Unit 15's incident table.

Before mainnet

  • Design canvas reviewed by someone hostile to the idea (lesson 1). A friendly review finds nothing.
  • Invariant and fork tests green, with formal checks on the core math (Unit 5, Unit 15).
  • Two independent audits, findings fixed and re-reviewed, then a contest (Unit 15).
  • Bug bounty live, sized to the value at risk (Unit 15).
  • Admin: a multisig with independent signers, timelocks, role separation, a written signing runbook (Units 5, 15).
  • Oracles: primary plus fallback, staleness and deviation guards, cost-of-attack documented (Unit 5).
  • Monitoring and automatic pause wired up, with a tested incident runbook (Unit 15).
  • Guarded launch: caps, a limited asset list, a schedule for raising caps (Unit 15).
  • Liquidity plan: market makers or a protocol vault, an incentive budget, retention metrics (Unit 13).
  • Legal review of the entity, the jurisdictions served, the token (if any), and the interface (Units 8, 13, 22).
  • Docs: risks disclosed plainly, contract addresses, audits, parameters.

After launch

  • Weekly risk review of parameters against current volatility and liquidity (Unit 15). Parameters that were right at launch drift out of date.
  • Public dashboards (fees, revenue, OI or TVL, bad debt), so users can see the protocol's health (Unit 16).
  • Progressive decentralisation: reduce admin powers as confidence grows, toward immutability (Units 5, 19).
  • Post-mortems for every incident, including near misses. The near miss is the free lesson.

Why "after launch" is half the list

Teams treat launch as the finish line, but Unit 15's lesson, security is a loop, not a snapshot, means launch is the start of the part that matters most. Parameters drift (what was a safe LTV in calm markets isn't after volatility rises), new collateral gets added, upgrades reintroduce risk, and attackers probe a live protocol harder than a testnet one. The weekly risk review and the progressive reduction of admin powers are what keep the protocol safe after the audit's snapshot has gone stale. A protocol that launches well and then stops doing the post-launch list is a protocol waiting for the parameter that drifted or the upgrade that wasn't re-audited.

Build it

Turn this checklist into a GitHub issue template in your capstone repo, and fill in an owner and a date for every item. Making each item a tracked issue with an owner converts the checklist from a nice idea into accountability, and the act of assigning a date to "two independent audits" or "monitoring with automatic pause" forces you to confront what they actually cost and how long they take, which is exactly the planning that distinguishes a protocol that launches safely from one that launches hopefully.
