Solana's yield stack mirrors Ethereum's, lending markets, liquid staking, and loops on top, but with Solana-specific risks that come from its architecture. The mechanics will be familiar from Units 7 and 14; the risks are new and worth knowing precisely, because they're where Solana yield strategies actually break. This lesson maps the stack and focuses on the chain-specific failure modes.

Lending

Kamino (the largest, with lending markets plus automated liquidity vaults), MarginFi, and Save (formerly Solend) are the main lending protocols. The mechanics are Unit 7's: utilisation-driven rate curves, LTVs, liquidations. Pyth and Switchboard supply the oracles (Unit 5). If you understand Aave, you understand these; the differences are operational, not conceptual.

Liquid staking

  • JitoSOL: stake rewards plus a share of Jito MEV tips (Unit 23), so it yields more than plain staking.
  • mSOL (Marinade) and Sanctum, which unifies liquidity across many LSTs so small LSTs can be swapped instantly, Sanctum is the "Sanctum unifies LST liquidity" layer that makes a long tail of LSTs viable.

Loops

The standard Solana loop: deposit JitoSOL, borrow SOL against it, stake the SOL into more JitoSOL, repeat (Unit 14). It works while the staking yield exceeds the SOL borrow cost, and it breaks on an LST depeg or a borrow-rate spike, exactly the looping fragility of Unit 14, on Solana assets.

The account-contention risk is the subtle one

On Ethereum, a liquidation cascade is bounded by gas and block space. On Solana, there's an extra bottleneck: many liquidators racing to liquidate positions often touch the same accounts (the market account, the oracle account), and because same-account writes can't run in parallel (Unit 23), the liquidations serialise exactly when you most need them fast. Combined with oracle lag during the congestion that a cascade causes, this is a Solana-specific amplifier of the liquidation-failure risk from Unit 7. When you assess a Solana lending market's safety, the question isn't just "are the parameters right?" but "will the liquidations actually clear fast enough when everyone's hitting the same hot accounts at once?"

Build it

For one Kamino market, record the LTV, liquidation threshold, oracle, and the program upgrade authority. Then simulate a JitoSOL/SOL loop at 5× and find the borrow rate at which it loses money (Unit 14's loop math). Recording the upgrade authority is the Solana-specific due-diligence step, many programs are upgradeable by a key, and you need to know who holds it before trusting the market, and finding the break-even borrow rate for the loop shows you how thin the margin is, which is the same fragility lesson as Unit 14 applied to real Solana assets.
