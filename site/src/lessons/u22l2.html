Privacy tools occupy the most legally fraught corner of DeFi, and the Tornado Cash cases are where the law is being written in real time. For anyone considering building privacy infrastructure, this history isn't optional background; it's the risk model. This lesson traces the cases from the 2022 sanctions through the 2024 court ruling to the ongoing criminal trials, and draws the practical lessons. It is not legal advice, and these cases are live, so verify the current status against primary sources.

The timeline

  • Aug 8, 2022: the US Treasury (OFAC) sanctioned Tornado Cash, including its immutable smart contracts, citing laundering by North Korea's Lazarus Group among others. Sanctioning code, not just people, was unprecedented.
  • Netherlands: developer Alexey Pertsev was arrested in 2022 and convicted of money laundering in May 2024 (64 months); he appealed.
  • Van Loon v. Treasury (Fifth Circuit, Nov 26, 2024): the court held that immutable smart contracts are not "property" that OFAC can block under its sanctions law, so the sanctions on the contracts exceeded Treasury's authority.
  • Mar 21, 2025: Treasury removed Tornado Cash from the sanctions list.
  • Roman Storm (co-founder, US): indicted in 2023. In August 2025 a jury convicted him of conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money-transmitting business and deadlocked on the money-laundering and sanctions charges. His acquittal motion was still pending in October 2026, and the retrial on the deadlocked counts was moved to April 2027.
  • Samourai Wallet's founders pleaded guilty in 2025 to unlicensed money transmission and were sentenced to prison.

What it means for builders

The live legal questions, none fully settled, are whether writing and publishing non-custodial code makes you a "money transmitter," and what duty a developer has to prevent misuse of a tool they don't control. The Van Loon ruling was a win for the principle that immutable code isn't sanctionable property, but Storm's conviction on the money-transmission count shows the developer can still face liability even when the contracts can't be sanctioned. And the answers differ by country, Pertsev was convicted in the Netherlands on money-laundering grounds that a US court might view differently.

Practical steps, not legal advice

If you're considering building privacy tools: get legal advice before you build, in every jurisdiction you'd touch. Avoid custody of user funds and fee capture that looks like a business you control, because those are what push a tool toward "money transmitter." Document the compliance features (lesson 3's association sets and view keys) that distinguish a privacy tool for honest users from a laundering service. And follow the Storm case, because its outcome will shape developer liability for non-custodial code for years. The field is genuinely unsettled, which means the risk is real and the honest answer to "is this legal?" is "it depends, and the law is actively changing."
