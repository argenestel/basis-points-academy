Shielded pools (lessons 1–3) are one privacy tool, but the broader toolbox has several techniques, each hiding a different thing and trusting a different thing. Knowing which to reach for is the point: you use ZK to prove a secret, FHE or MPC to compute on many parties' secrets, TEEs when you need speed and can accept hardware trust, and encrypted mempools when the thing you want to hide is transaction order. This lesson is that toolbox, organised by what each technique hides and trusts.

The toolbox

What each technique hides, and what it trusts

Zero-knowledge proofs (circom, Noir, zkVMs)hides: the inputs to a computationtrusts: math (plus a trusted setup for some SNARKs). Use: shielded pools, private L2s, credentials
Fully homomorphic encryption (Zama's fhEVM)hides: data while it's being computed ontrusts: math plus key holders (threshold decryption). Use: confidential tokens, sealed bids
Multi-party computationhides: each party's input from the otherstrusts: an honest threshold of parties. Use: wallet key shares, private auctions
Trusted execution environments (SGX, TDX)hides: data inside the enclavetrusts: the hardware vendor and attestation. Use: Flashbots BuilderNet, private orderflow
Encrypted mempools (Shutter)hides: transactions until they're orderedtrusts: a threshold keyper set. Use: MEV and front-running protection

The key distinction

ZK proves something about data without revealing it, but it doesn't let you compute on other people's hidden data. For that you need FHE (compute on encrypted data directly) or MPC (several parties jointly compute without revealing their inputs). This is the difference between "prove my bid is under my budget without revealing it" (ZK) and "compute the winning bid across everyone's sealed bids without revealing any" (FHE/MPC). Encrypted mempools solve a different problem entirely: not hiding data forever, but hiding transaction order until it's committed, which defeats front-running and sandwiching (Unit 11) because a searcher can't act on a transaction it can't read.

A note on the newer tech's maturity

On Solana, Token-2022's confidential transfers use ZK proofs to hide amounts (Unit 23). The proof program had a critical bug found and patched in 2025, a reminder that privacy cryptography is hard to implement correctly, and that a bug in the proof system can silently break the privacy guarantee it promises. The same caution applies to FHE (still slow and new), MPC (depends on the honest-threshold assumption holding), and TEEs (hardware vulnerabilities have broken enclave guarantees before). These are powerful tools, but they're newer and less battle-tested than signatures and hashes, so check the current security status before you build something that relies on one.

Choosing

The decision procedure: need to prove something without revealing it? ZK. Need to compute on secrets from many users (a sealed-bid auction, a private order book)? FHE or MPC, and accept the cost. Need speed today and can accept hardware trust? TEEs. Need fair ordering? Encrypt transactions until they're ordered. Each technique is the right answer to a specific requirement, and using the wrong one (ZK where you needed MPC) means the thing you wanted to build simply doesn't work.

Build it

Write the same "sealed-bid auction" three ways on paper: commit-reveal (the simple baseline), ZK (prove each bid ≤ budget without revealing it), and FHE (compute the max on encrypted bids directly). Compare their trust, cost and latency. Working the same problem three ways is the clearest way to feel the distinction: commit-reveal needs a reveal phase (and bidders can drop out), ZK proves properties but can't compute the max across bids, and FHE computes the max but is slow and trusts the key holders. The comparison is the choosing procedure, made concrete.
