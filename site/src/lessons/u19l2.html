Governance attacks are instructive because each one is a specific defence that was missing, and the defences are exactly the features of lesson 1 and lesson 3. This lesson walks five attacks, from the famous Beanstalk flash-loan drain to the 2026 BonkDAO treasury theft, as a catalogue of what happens when a governance system skips checkpointing, timelocks, payload verification or realistic quorums. It's a defender's tour: each attack names the control that would have stopped it.

Five attacks

Governance attacks and the missing defence

Beanstalk (Apr 2022, ~$182M)flash-borrowed ~$1B, deposited for ~79% voting power, passed a malicious proposal through an "emergency commit" path in one transaction; kept ~$80M after repayingvoting power from same-block deposits; an emergency path with no delay
Tornado Cash governance (May 2023)a proposal that looked like an approved one hid a self-destruct; after it passed, the attacker redeployed different code at the same address (CREATE2) granting ~1.2M votesverifying the deployed payload at execution; metamorphic-contract checks
Compound Proposal 289 (Jul 2024)a whale-led group accumulated delegation and passed a proposal moving ~499k COMP (~$24M) to a vault they controlled; ended in a negotiated settlementquorum and turnout design, delegate engagement
Small Solana DAO (2025, ~$230k)ten identical-looking proposals; nine empty, the tenth sent the treasury to the attackerproposal simulation and review
BonkDAO (Jul 2026, ~$20M)gathered ~1% of BONK supply, enough to clear the approval threshold on Realms, and executed a treasury transfer with no timelockexecution timelock, realistic quorums, monitoring

The patterns

Line them up and the recurring themes are clear:

  • Low turnout and low thresholds. BonkDAO needed only ~1% of supply because turnout was low enough that 1% cleared the bar. Many DAOs have treasuries that outlived their communities, "sleepy DAOs" whose quorums are trivially met by an attacker (lesson 3's sunset recommendation).
  • Emergency paths with no delay. Beanstalk's "emergency commit" let a proposal pass and execute in one transaction, defeating the whole point of a timelock. An emergency path that bypasses the delay is a backdoor.
  • Payloads nobody simulated. The Solana DAO's nine empty proposals plus one drainer, and Tornado's metamorphic redeploy, both relied on nobody actually checking what the proposal did. A proposal that isn't simulated is a signature on a blank cheque.
  • Current-balance voting. Beanstalk counted same-block deposits, so a flash loan was voting power. Checkpointing (lesson 1) kills this.

Monitoring catches what design misses

Even with good design, monitoring is the backstop. Services like Hypernative watch proposals and flag treasury transfers in real time, and a watchful community caught a near-identical attack on another DAO before BonkDAO's happened. The lesson: governance security is design (checkpoints, timelocks, realistic quorums) plus vigilance (someone actually reading and simulating every proposal before it executes). The timelock exists precisely to give that vigilance time to act, which is why the attacks that skipped the timelock were the ones that succeeded instantly.

Build it

Reproduce a Beanstalk-style attack against a toy Governor in Foundry: a governance token that counts current balances, an emergencyExecute with no delay, and a flash loan. Then fix it with checkpoints and a timelock and show the attack fails. Building the vulnerable version first, flash-borrow, vote, drain, all in one transaction, and then watching the checkpoint and timelock defeat it, is the most direct way to understand why those two features are non-negotiable, and it's the same reproduce-then-defend loop as Unit 18.
