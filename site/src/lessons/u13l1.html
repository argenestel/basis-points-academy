Before you write a token's economics (lesson 2), you have to fund the team that builds the protocol, and crypto has its own ladder of funding instruments, most of which involve a token that doesn't exist yet. This lesson walks that ladder in the order teams actually climb it, with the terms you'll be offered, and sets up the central decision every crypto project faces: are you selling equity in a company, a token in a network, or both?

The ladder

  • Grants: ecosystem foundations, L2 grant programs, retroactive public-goods funding. Non-dilutive and good for credibility, but small, and they come with ecosystem strings.
  • Angels and seed VC: usually equity (a SAFE) plus a token warrant or side letter giving investors a share of future tokens. Typical terms: a 1-year cliff and 2–4 years of vesting (Unit 13 lesson 2 covers why cliffs and vesting matter).
  • Strategic rounds from exchanges, market makers or other protocols: they bring distribution and liquidity, but can bring conflicts of interest too.
  • Public token sales: the ICOs of 2017 gave way to IEOs and launchpads, then LBPs, then, in 2025, a return of compliant onchain raises.

After years where public token sales were legally radioactive in much of the world, 2025 saw them return in more compliant forms, with KYC, structure, and backing from regulated players like Coinbase. For a founder, this reopens a path that was effectively closed: raising from a crowd rather than only from VCs.

The revenue-first alternative

Not every protocol takes the VC ladder. Hyperliquid famously took no VC money at all: it built a product people paid to use, generated revenue, and then airdropped about 31% of supply to its users in November 2024 (Unit 13 lesson 5). This is a genuinely different model: instead of selling the future to investors, you build something that earns, and you distribute ownership to the people who actually use it. It's harder (you need a product that earns before you have funding), but it avoids the overhang and the conflicts that come with a cap table full of investors waiting to sell (Unit 13 lesson 2).

Decide what you're selling

Equity, token, or both

The decision under all of this: are you selling equity in a company, a token in a network, or both? This determines your legal structure (a foundation or DAO entity alongside a dev company), your jurisdiction, and your disclosures. Investors usually want both (equity for the company's value, a token warrant for the network's), which is why the SAFE-plus-warrant structure is standard. But the two can conflict, a company optimising for equity value may not be optimising for the network's, and getting the structure wrong early is expensive to fix. Get a crypto-specialist lawyer before any sale. This is not legal advice.

Build it

Write a one-page funding plan for your capstone protocol (Unit 17): how much you need for 18 months, which instruments you'd use, what the investor terms would be (valuation, token allocation, cliff and vesting), and what you'd keep for the community. Forcing yourself to name a specific valuation and vesting schedule, and to decide the community allocation, turns "we'll raise some money" into the concrete trade-offs this lesson describes, and it feeds directly into the token design of the next one.
