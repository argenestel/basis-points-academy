Zero-knowledge proofs sound like pure cryptography, but for a DeFi engineer the important facts are practical: what a proof actually guarantees, what it costs to make and to check, and where you'd reach for one. You don't need to understand the polynomial commitments to use a zkVM, just as you don't need to understand elliptic curves to use a signature. This lesson is the working engineer's model of ZK.

What a proof guarantees

A succinct proof convinces a verifier that a computation was done correctly, in far less time than it would take to redo the computation. The "zero-knowledge" part adds that the proof can hide the inputs, but most blockchain uses care about the succinctness (cheap to verify) more than the privacy. The magic is the asymmetry: proving is expensive, verifying is cheap and roughly constant, so you can prove a huge computation once and let anyone verify it with a few hundred thousand gas.

The families

SNARKs, STARKs, zkVMs

SNARKs (Groth16, PLONK-family)tiny proofs, cheap on-chain verificationsome need a trusted setup (a one-time ceremony that must not be compromised)
STARKsno trusted setup, hash-based (plausibly post-quantum), larger proofsoften wrapped in a SNARK for cheap L1 verification
zkVMs (SP1, RISC Zero)write normal Rust, prove its RISC-V executionmade ZK accessible to application engineers, not just cryptographers

The zkVM is the one that changed who can use this. Before zkVMs, using ZK meant writing your computation as an arithmetic circuit in a specialised language, which only cryptographers could do well. A zkVM lets you write ordinary Rust, run it, and get a proof that it executed correctly. That's the difference between ZK being a research tool and an engineering tool.

Where you'll use proofs

  • ZK rollups and verifiable order-book matching (Lighter, Unit 2): prove the whole batch of transactions executed correctly, so L1 doesn't have to re-run them.
  • ZK light-client bridges (Unit 12): prove "this header was finalised by the source chain's validators," so chain B can verify A's consensus cheaply, the trust-minimised bridge.
  • Coprocessors: prove a computation over historical chain data off-chain and verify it on-chain, e.g. a 30-day TWAP or a user's lifetime trading volume, things too expensive to compute in a contract.
  • Privacy (Unit 22): shielded pools and private order flow, where the zero-knowledge property hides the inputs, not just compresses the verification.

The cost model

prover time ≫ execution time (often 1,000×+ for general zkVMs, improving fast) verifier time ≈ constant on-chain verification ≈ a few hundred thousand gas (SNARK)

The whole point: you pay a lot once to prove, and then everyone verifies cheaply forever. This asymmetry is what makes rollups and light clients work.

The practical implication for a builder: ZK is worth it when a computation is verified far more often than it's run, or when it's too expensive to re-run on-chain at all. A rollup batch is verified by all of Ethereum but proven once, so the economics work. A one-off computation that's cheap to redo is not worth proving.

Build it

With SP1 or RISC Zero, prove a Rust program that computes the Merkle root of 1,000 balances (Unit 2), then verify the proof in a Foundry test on a local chain. Record the prover time and the verification gas. Seeing a proof take seconds to generate but verify in a few hundred thousand gas, for a computation over a thousand items, is the cost-model asymmetry made concrete, and it's the moment ZK stops being abstract and becomes a tool you can reach for.
