A lending protocol doesn't know what anything is worth. It asks an oracle, and then it acts on the answer as if it were true: it lets you borrow, it liquidates you, it mints against your collateral. So the oracle is not a detail; it is the protocol's entire grip on reality, and if an attacker can make the oracle lie cheaply, the protocol will hand over its funds while behaving exactly as designed. This lesson is about choosing and hardening a price feed so that it can't be turned into a withdrawal.

Push, pull, and DEX oracles

  • Push oracles (Chainlink Data Feeds): a decentralised network posts a new price on-chain whenever it deviates past a threshold or a heartbeat interval elapses. Simple to read, battle-tested, but they can lag in a fast market, and you must check how old the posted price is.
  • Pull oracles (Pyth, Chainlink Data Streams): a signed price is fetched off-chain and verified on-chain in the same transaction that uses it. Fresher and cheaper to maintain, but you must verify the signature, check the timestamp, and respect the confidence interval the feed provides.
  • DEX spot price (reading a pool's reserves): the most convenient and the most dangerous. A pool's spot price can be moved within one transaction by anyone with a flash loan (Unit 7). Never use it for anything that matters.
  • DEX TWAP (Uniswap v3's cumulative tick): a time-weighted average over a window. Manipulation-resistant over long windows because you'd have to hold the price wrong across many blocks, but it lags, and on a thin pool, or on a chain where one proposer can control consecutive blocks, even a TWAP can be moved.
  • Validator-posted (Hyperliquid): validators submit external prices and the protocol takes a weighted median. Decentralised among the validator set.

Why DEX spot is a trap

Recall the cost-of-corruption arithmetic from Unit 1: moving a thin pool's price 20% costs only the round-trip slippage, often a few tens of thousands of dollars, and a flash loan means the attacker doesn't even need the capital. If your protocol reads that spot price to value collateral, the attacker moves it, borrows against the inflated value, and the loan they walk away with dwarfs the cost of the move.

Mango Markets, October 2022

Mango valued traders' collateral from the spot price of MNGO on the thin markets its oracle read. An attacker took a large long MNGO perp position, then bought MNGO across those thin markets, pushing the price up several-fold. Their own position, now marked at the inflated price, counted as enormous collateral, and they borrowed out more than $100M of the protocol's other assets against it, then left. The price pump cost a fraction of what was borrowed. The contract did precisely what it was written to do; it just believed a price the attacker controlled. The lesson is one sentence: never let a trader's collateral be valued from a market that same trader can cheaply move.

The hardening checklist

Whatever source you choose, wrap it in these checks. Each one closes a specific failure.

require(block.timestamp - updatedAt <= maxStaleness); // the price isn't old require(answer > 0); // sane value require(confidence / price <= maxConfPct); // pull oracles: the feed isn't uncertain borrowCap[asset] = f(realLiquidityAt2pct, realLiquidityAt5pct); // can't over-borrow against thin depth useMedianOf([primaryFeed, fallbackFeed]); // no single point of failure

Staleness and a positive-value check are the minimum. The liquidity-based borrow cap is what makes even a true price move survivable: you can't borrow more against an asset than the market could liquidate.

Choosing per asset

There is no universal right oracle; it depends on the asset and the action. A deep, blue-chip asset can use a push feed with a tight heartbeat. A long-tail asset may have no good feed at all, which is itself the answer: if you can't price it safely, don't accept it as collateral. For anything whose price could be moved within a transaction, assume it will be, and either use a manipulation-resistant source (a decentralised feed, a long TWAP) or cap the exposure so that a successful manipulation isn't profitable. The question to ask of every price your protocol reads is the cost-of-corruption inequality: what does it cost to move this number, and what can an attacker get by moving it (Unit 1)?

Build it

On a mainnet fork, build a toy lending pool that prices collateral from a thin Uniswap pool's spot price, then write the exploit that drains it with a flash loan: pump the pool, deposit the now-overvalued collateral, borrow the pool dry. This is the Mango shape in miniature, run against your own local contract, which is the only place it's legal to run it. Then fix it: swap the spot read for a Chainlink feed plus a liquidity-based borrow cap, and show the same exploit now fails. Seeing your own pool drained and then made safe is the fastest way to never ship a spot-price oracle.
