A token's design decides whether it's an asset people want to hold or a slow-motion dump on retail. The arithmetic is simple and the traps are well-known, yet projects keep walking into them because the incentives push toward a high headline valuation and a low initial float. This lesson is the token-design math (float, FDV, unlocks, sell pressure) and the one question that separates a token-as-product from a token-as-fundraise.

The definitions

FDV = price × max supply market cap = price × circulating supply float = circulating / max supply annual sell pressure ≈ emissions + unlocks × (share expected to sell) value accrual ≈ fees routed to buyback/burn or stakers − dilution

FDV is what the token is "worth" if every token existed today; market cap is what's actually liquid. The gap between them is the overhang waiting to unlock.

The low-float/high-FDV trap

The dominant complaint of 2024 was "low float, high FDV," and it's worth working through because it's a trap you can design yourself into without meaning to.

Why low float / high FDV dumps on buyers

Launch10% circulating, FDV $10Bmarket cap $1B, looks "cheap" vs FDV
Team + investor tokensthe other 90%, vesting over 3–4 yearsa wall of future supply
Each monthunlocks add to circulatingnew supply must be absorbed by new demand
If demand doesn't growprice falls as float rises toward 100%early public buyers absorb the unlocks, at a loss

The structure almost guarantees that public buyers at launch are buying the top, because they're buying 10% of the supply at a valuation that implies the other 90% is worth the same, and then that 90% arrives over years. The fix is an honest float and a clear, not-too-aggressive unlock schedule, so buyers can see the supply coming and price it.

Ways a token can accrue value

A token is only an asset if holding it has a reason. The ways (Unit 6's fee switch, Unit 21's ve-models):

  • Fee switch and buyback-burn: Uniswap's UNIfication (Dec 2025) turned on protocol fees for burns; Hyperliquid routes trading fees to buy back HYPE.
  • Fee sharing to stakers, usually with a lock-up.
  • Work token: you must stake it to provide a service (validators, keepers, HIP-3 deployers staking HYPE). Demand to hold comes from needing to work.
  • Governance over valuable parameters and a treasury (Unit 19).
  • Collateral and gas use on your own chain.

The one question

Who needs to hold your token, and why?

If the honest answer is "nobody, but we hope speculators will," then your token is a fundraising instrument wearing a product's clothes, and its price will track hype, not usage. A token-as-product has a group that needs to hold it: validators who must stake, users who get fee discounts, governors who steer a valuable treasury. Ask the question plainly, and if you can't name the holders and their reason, ask whether you need a token at all (Unit 17's design canvas asks exactly this). The second test: what happens to the price if emissions stop? If the answer is "it collapses," the emissions were the product.

Build it

Model your token in a spreadsheet: allocations (team, investors, community, treasury), cliffs and vesting, emissions, and a fee-funded buyback. Chart monthly net supply change (emissions plus unlocks minus buybacks) against buyback capacity at three revenue scenarios. Seeing the months where net supply is strongly positive, the unlock cliffs where a year's vesting hits at once, is how you catch a dumping schedule before you commit to it, and how you size a buyback that could actually offset it.
